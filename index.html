The efficient option

Why heat pumps make sense in the South Bay.

Heat pumps don't burn fuel to make heat — they move heat from one place to another, which is why they can deliver several units of heating for every unit of electricity they use. In a climate without deep freezes, that efficiency holds up all winter.

The Bay Area is close to the ideal case: mild winters, moderate summers, and no extended sub-freezing stretches where older heat pumps used to struggle. One system handles both seasons, so there's no separate furnace to maintain.

  • One system for heating and cooling
  • No gas combustion in the house
  • Well matched to the Bay Area's mild climate
  • Ductless options where there's no ductwork

Common questions

Good to know

Is a heat pump a good fit for a Bay Area home?

Usually yes. The South Bay climate is mild on both ends — we rarely see deep freezes or extreme heat — which is exactly the range heat pumps are most efficient in. One system handles both heating and cooling, and because it moves heat rather than burning gas it typically costs less to run. We'll look at your home and tell you honestly whether it makes sense for your situation.

What's a ductless mini-split, and when does it make sense?

A mini-split is a heat pump that doesn't need ductwork — an outdoor unit connects to one or more indoor wall units by a small line set. They're a strong option for additions, converted garages, ADUs, upstairs rooms that never get comfortable, and older homes with no ducts where running new ductwork would mean tearing into walls and ceilings.

Should I repair my existing system or replace it?

It depends on the age of the equipment, what failed, and what the repair costs relative to a replacement. A newer system with a failed capacitor is worth fixing. A twenty-year-old furnace with a cracked heat exchanger isn't. We'll tell you which situation you're in, including when the cheaper repair is the right answer.

How often should HVAC equipment be serviced?

Once a year. A tune-up catches the things that cause mid-season failures — weak capacitors, low refrigerant charge, dirty coils, clogged filters and loose electrical connections — and keeps the system running at the efficiency you paid for.
